Setting Up Your Vehicle for a Safer Delivery

Pre-Drive Checks

Every safe delivery starts before you turn the ignition. Take a few minutes to check your vehicle before each run.

Tyres: Check pressure with a gauge — match the manufacturer's recommendation (usually on a sticker inside the driver's door). Legal minimum tread depth in Belgium is 1.6mm. Inspect for cuts, bulges, or embedded objects.

Lights: Verify headlights (dipped and full beam), tail lights, brake lights, indicators, fog lights, and reverse lights. A broken light means an on-the-spot fine in Belgium.

Fluids: Check engine oil (dipstick between min/max), coolant (when engine is cold), brake fluid, and windscreen washer fluid.

Brakes: Test gently when starting to move. They should feel firm, not spongy. Grinding, squealing, or pulling to one side needs immediate attention.

Mirrors & Windscreen: Clean, properly adjusted, and free from cracks. A cracked windscreen can fail inspection.

Warning Lights: Engine, brake, or oil pressure lights — don't ignore them. Get checked before driving.

Belgium's Vehicle Inspection Requirements

⚠ Warning

As of September 2026, rules vary by region:

  • Flanders: Inspection every 2 years, regardless of age or mileage.
  • Wallonia & Brussels: Two-year cycles generally limited to cars under 8 years old with less than 110,000 km.

If your vehicle fails, you have 2 months (Flanders) to fix and re-inspect. Keep your inspection certificate in the car.

Insurance & Documentation

Carry your insurance Green Card and a standard accident report form (constat amiable) at all times. In Belgium, the vehicle is insured, not the driver. Driving without valid insurance is illegal.

Understanding Belgium's Road Rules

Speed Limits

Road Type Limit
Motorways 120 km/h nationwide
Built-up areas 50 km/h default; Brussels enforces 30 km/h citywide
Rural roads Flanders: 70 km/h; Wallonia: 90 km/h
Residential zones (woonerf) 20 km/h
School/urban Zone 30 30 km/h

Speed cameras are common. Don't rely on GPS speed limits — watch for signs.

Parking: Colour-Coded Zones

✔ Safety Tip

Brussels parking zones:

  • Blue Zone: Free with blue parking disc, 1–2 hour limit
  • Green Zone: Paid visitor zone, no time limit
  • Red/Orange Zone: Paid, max 2 hours — ideal for quick deliveries
  • Grey Zone: Paid, max 4.5 hours

As of September 2026: perpendicular parking only where explicitly signed. Otherwise, park parallel to the curb.

Low Emission Zones (LEZs)

⚠ Warning

Brussels, Antwerp, and Ghent enforce LEZs restricting older diesel and petrol vehicles. Compliance is monitored by automatic number plate recognition. Check your vehicle before entering — fines apply even if you didn't know about restrictions.

Category B License for Electric Vans

Drivers with a Category B (car) license can operate zero-emission vans up to 4.25 tonnes MAM (vs the traditional 3.5-tonne limit). Full effect from November 2027; interim measures already in place.

Right-of-Way & Intersections

  • At unmarked intersections, traffic from the right has priority
  • On roundabouts, yield to traffic already circulating
  • When turning, yield to pedestrians and cyclists continuing straight
  • From September 2026, some intersections feature "square green" signals allowing cyclists and pedestrians to cross simultaneously

Common Urban Hazards

  • Tram tracks: Trams always have priority; tracks are slippery and can catch tyres at shallow angles
  • Cobblestones: Bumpy and slippery when wet — slow down
  • Cyclists & pedestrians: Check blind spots before turning or opening doors
  • Narrow streets: Historic centres have tight lanes and limited visibility — drive slowly

Safe Use of Delivery Equipment

Thermal Bags Placement

Never put thermal bags on the passenger seat — they can slide forward and interfere with controls during sudden braking. Place them on the floor behind the front seats or in the boot, secured so they don't shift.

Loading Your Thermal Bag

Place heavy items at the bottom, lightest on top. This creates a stable centre of gravity. Separate hot food from cold items where possible.

⚠ Warning

Using a handheld phone while driving is illegal in Belgium and carries an on-the-spot fine. Use a fixed dashboard or windscreen holder. Never hold your phone, type messages, or browse the app while the car is moving. Pull over to a safe, legal place before using your phone.

Combatting Fatigue for Car Drivers

Why Fatigue Matters

Fatigue contributes to 20–30% of road accidents in Belgium, with higher rates on motorways. Going without proper sleep affects your brain similarly to being drunk — slower reactions, cloudy judgment, reduced hazard awareness.

What Causes Fatigue

  • Not enough quality sleep — your body needs proper recovery
  • Time of day — late-night, early-morning, and mid-afternoon runs are hardest
  • Long, monotonous driving — back-to-back deliveries on motorways drain you fast

Warning Signs

  • Constant yawning
  • Struggling to focus
  • Sore, heavy eyes or blurred vision
  • Slow reactions to brake lights or signals
  • Zoning out — can't remember the last few kilometres
  • Drifting out of your lane
  • Micro-sleeps — eyes closing for 1–2 seconds
  • Missing your exit

If any of these happen, pull over immediately.

Prevention

✔ Safety Tip
  • Get 7–8 hours of sleep before your run
  • Be extra cautious during high-risk hours (late night, early morning)
  • Take a 15-minute break every 2 hours — a 15–20 minute nap restores alertness better than caffeine
  • Eat balanced meals, not heavy greasy ones
  • Check medications for drowsiness side effects
  • Plan routes with rest stops on long stretches
  • Stay hydrated — dehydration accelerates fatigue

Setting Up Your Motorcycle/Moped

Pre-Ride Checks

Every safe delivery starts before you turn the ignition. Check your bike before each run.

Tyres: Check pressure against manufacturer's recommendation. Under-inflated tyres reduce grip, especially in wet conditions. Legal minimum tread: 1.6mm. Inspect for cuts, cracks, or wear.

Brakes: Squeeze the front brake lever and push forward — the front wheel should lock. Repeat for rear brake. Check brake fluid on hydraulic systems. Worn pads or spongy levers need immediate attention.

Lights: Headlight, tail light, brake light, and indicators. Lights are mandatory at night and in poor visibility. Test your horn.

Chain: Should have 20–30mm of slack in the middle. Too tight or too loose can snap or damage the gearbox. Keep lubricated, especially after wet weather.

Oil: Check via sight glass or dipstick. Low oil can damage your engine mid-delivery.

Mirrors: Clean and properly adjusted. Ensure chassis number and license plate are visible. Check fuel level.

Inspection Requirements

Motorcycles over 125cc must undergo technical inspection before second-hand sale or after an accident. Mopeds and bikes under 125cc are currently exempt from periodic inspections, but maintenance remains your responsibility.

Battery & Electrical Safety

Warning Signs of Battery Damage

⚠ Warning

Stop using or charging immediately if you notice:

  • Battery has swelled or changed shape
  • Leaks or unusual moisture
  • Excessively hot during charging or use
  • Visible scratches, dents, or cracks
  • Discolouration or colour change
  • Strong chemical or burning odour

A damaged lithium-ion battery can undergo "thermal runaway" — rapid overheating and fire that is extremely difficult to extinguish.

Charging Safely

✔ Safety Tip
  • Use only the manufacturer's charger — a different charger can cause overheating
  • Charge in a ventilated area, away from flammable materials
  • Don't charge overnight or when you're not home
  • Check cables and connections for damage regularly
  • Keep a working smoke alarm nearby

Storing Spare Batteries

Store in a cool, dry place away from sunlight and heat. Fire-resistant battery storage bags provide extra protection.

What to Do in a Battery Fire

⚠ Warning

Evacuate immediately and call 112. Do not attempt to extinguish a large lithium-ion fire yourself. For a very small, just-starting fire, use a CO2 extinguisher — never use water on an active electrical fire unless the power source is disconnected.

Disposing of Damaged Batteries

Never throw lithium-ion batteries in household waste. In Belgium, dispose via Bebat collection points (check their website for locations). Let staff know if the battery is damaged or swollen.

Protect Yourself While You Ride

Helmets — Your Most Important Protection

Every rider and passenger must wear a certified helmet with an ECE approval mark (ECE 22.05 or newer ECE 22.06). The helmet should fit snugly; chin strap fastened every ride. Replace every 3–5 years or immediately after any impact.

Mandatory Protective Clothing (Motorcycles over 50cc)

Belgian law requires:

  • Gloves — full coverage, no fingerless
  • Long-sleeved jacket — appropriate motorcycle material
  • Long trousers or overall — full leg coverage
  • Boots or ankle-boots — ankle protection

For mopeds (50cc and under), only a helmet is legally required, but gloves, jacket, trousers, and boots are strongly recommended.

High-Visibility Clothing

A high-vis vest meeting EN ISO 20471 standards should be worn over your jacket, especially in low light or poor weather.

Eye Protection & Seasonal Layers

Wear shatterproof eye protection or a helmet with visor. In winter: thermal base layers, windproof mid-layer, waterproof outer jacket. In summer: breathable, vented motorcycle gear.

Motorcycle/Moped Road Safety

Lane Filtering Rules

Filtering between slow-moving or stopped traffic is legal in Belgium under strict conditions:

  • Maximum filtering speed: 50 km/h
  • Speed difference vs surrounding traffic: max 20 km/h
  • On multi-lane roads: filter between the two leftmost lanes
  • Return to your lane when traffic exceeds 50 km/h
  • Emergency lane use allowed, but clear immediately for priority vehicles
  • Filtering prohibited near pedestrian/cyclist crossings
  • You may activate all indicators simultaneously for visibility

Road Positioning & Overtaking

Position yourself about two-thirds into the lane for maximum visibility and safety. Overtake on the left, signal clearly. Never overtake on solid white lines. Give cyclists at least 1m clearance in built-up areas, 1.5m outside.

Right-of-Way

At unmarked intersections, traffic from the right has priority. On roundabouts, yield to traffic already circulating. When turning, yield to cyclists and pedestrians continuing straight.

Common Road Hazards

  • Tram tracks: Extremely slippery when wet; cross at close to 90 degrees; trams always have priority
  • Cobblestones: Dangerously slippery when wet; reduce speed, keep balanced, ride the middle line
  • Painted markings & manhole covers: Slick in wet conditions — avoid braking or leaning on them
  • Door zones: Maintain 1–1.5m from parked cars
  • Intersections: Approach cautiously, make eye contact, use clear signals
  • Weather: Wet leaves, ice, and snow drastically reduce traction

Adverse Weather Riding

Expect Rain

Belgium averages 12–15 rainy days per month, year-round. The trick isn't avoiding rain — it's learning to ride safely in it.

Visibility First

Use headlight and tail light even during the day in grey or wet conditions. Keep your visor clean; use anti-fog treatment or pin-lock insert. Wear bright, reflective clothing or high-vis vest.

Adjust How You Ride

  • Slow down — especially when cornering; dry-weather lean angles are dangerous on wet roads
  • Brake earlier and more gently — use both brakes; avoid harsh front-brake use
  • Increase following distance — at least a 3-second gap
  • Watch for specific hazards — painted lines, manhole covers, metal plates, tram tracks all become dangerously slippery. Cross tram tracks close to 90 degrees with no braking or accelerating.
  • Cobblestones in rain — slow right down, keep relaxed, avoid sudden inputs
  • Puddles hide potholes — slow down; ease off brakes through puddles; test brakes gently afterward

When Ice Appears

Freezing rain, black ice, and frost appear in shaded areas, on bridges, and early mornings. Check RMI weather warnings before riding. Seriously consider whether riding is worth the risk — motorcycles have far less traction than cars on ice. If you do ride: use the highest gear possible, avoid sudden inputs, keep speed very low.

Wet-Weather Gear

Waterproof jacket and trousers (or one-piece suit) are non-negotiable. Waterproof gloves maintain dexterity and grip. Waterproof boots keep feet dry and warm. Ensure visor seals properly; consider breath guard or anti-fog coating.

Setting Up Your E-Bike for a Safer Ride

The 3-Minute Pre-Start Check

Tyres: Give them a firm squeeze — they should feel solid, not soft. Check the pressure marking on the side. Under-inflated tyres make steering harder and increase puncture risk.

Seat: Adjust to the right height; confirm it's inserted at least 5cm into the frame. A loose seat can shift unexpectedly while riding.

Wheels & Brakes: Lift the handlebars and spin the front wheel — it should be secure and spin freely without rubbing brake pads. Test both brakes for quick, smooth response. Check gears shift without sticking or grinding.

Essential Gear for Every Ride

  • Helmet — not legally required for standard e-bikes, but strongly recommended
  • High-visibility vest — so drivers can see you, especially in poor light
  • Enclosed shoes — protect feet and give pedal grip
  • Mobile phone holder — handling your phone while riding is illegal and dangerous
  • Waterproof clothing — be prepared for Belgium's weather

Choosing the Right E-Bike for Deliveries

Belgium's E-Bike Laws

⚠ Warning

Standard e-bikes (pedelecs) are classified as bicycles. Your e-bike's motor must not exceed 250W. Anything more powerful is classified as a speed pedelec or moped — requiring a license, registration, license plate, and mandatory helmet.

Battery-assisted power must automatically cut off at 25 km/h. If your bike continues assisting beyond that speed, it's not road-legal and you could face fines.

Never Modify Your E-Bike

Tampering with motor or speed settings makes your bike illegal without registration and a license. It also makes the bike harder to control, increases braking distances, and voids insurance. Always keep your e-bike in original manufacturer settings.

Required Lighting & Reflectors

✔ Safety Tip

When riding after sunset or in poor visibility, Belgian law requires:

  • White or yellow front light, red rear light (visible and working)
  • White front reflector, red rear reflector
  • Yellow/orange pedal reflectors
  • Reflective elements on wheels

Lights can be attached to clothing or bag, but fixing quality lights to your bike is recommended. Riding without reflectors can result in fines of around €58.

Lithium-Ion Battery Safety

Warning Signs of Battery Damage

⚠ Warning

Stop using or charging immediately if you notice:

  • Battery has swelled or changed shape
  • Leaks coming from the battery
  • Unusually hot while charging or in use
  • Visible scratches, dents, or cracks
  • Colour change or discolouration
  • Strong chemical or burning odour

Charging Safely

✔ Safety Tip
  • Use only the manufacturer's charger — a different charger can cause overheating or fire
  • Don't charge on or near flammable surfaces (beds, sofas, wooden furniture)
  • Charge away from living areas where possible (garage, utility room)
  • Don't charge overnight or when you're not home
  • Keep a working smoke alarm nearby
  • Keep the area around the battery clear — don't block escape routes

Storing Spare Batteries

Use battery bags designed to protect against moisture and impacts. Some are fire-resistant and can slow or contain a fire.

Disposing of Damaged Batteries

Never throw lithium-ion batteries in household waste. In Belgium, dispose via Bebat (over 22,000 collection points — recycling centres, bike shops, retail stores). If the battery is swollen or leaking, let staff know. If the battery can't be removed from the e-bike, take the whole bike to a specialised recycling centre.

Protect Yourself While You Ride

Helmet — Your Essential Protection

Not legally required for standard e-bikes, but the most important safety decision you can make. Choose one meeting EN 1078. Fasten every ride. Replace every 3–5 years or immediately after any impact.

High-Visibility Outer-Wear

Wear a high-vis vest or jacket meeting EN ISO 20471 standards, fastened at the front. Fluorescent materials for daytime visibility; retroreflective strips for night. Being visible dramatically reduces collision risk.

Eye Protection

Shatterproof safety-style sunglasses protect from dust, wind, rain, and debris. Keep lenses clean and scratch-free.

Seasonal Protection

  • Summer: Apply sunscreen every 2–4 hours, even on cloudy days. Long-sleeved shirt under high-vis for extra UV protection.
  • Winter: Neck gaiter for cold wind. At least three layers for insulation. Waterproof jacket and trousers essential — wet clothing reduces body temperature, concentration, and reaction time.

Footwear

Avoid open-toe shoes. Use non-slip, covered shoes with secure pedal grip — especially important in wet conditions.

Road Safety & Lane Selection

Right-of-Way Rules

  • At unmarked intersections, traffic from your right has priority
  • On continuous cycle paths crossing a road, drivers should yield to you — but don't assume they will. Make eye contact.
  • On roundabouts, yield to traffic already inside; motorised vehicles inside must yield to cyclists also circulating
  • Zebra crossings are for pedestrians — dismount and walk your bike across to get right of way
  • In bicycle streets (fietsstraat), cars are guests — they can't overtake you and are limited to 30 km/h
  • Red means stop, even on a bike

Cycling Infrastructure Types

Type Sign/Marking Rules
Fietspad (cycle path) Blue sign D7 or white lines Legally required to use if on your right; never use one on the left side
Fietsstrook (cycle lane) Road markings Same rules as fietspad; cars can't stop, park, or drive on it
Non-mandatory path Sign R12 Your choice — use it or ride on the road
Suggestion lane (fietssuggestiestrook) Coloured strip Not a legal cycle path; cars can drive, stop, and park on it
Shared path Pedestrian + bike sign Pedestrians have priority; slow down, use your bell

Where to Position Yourself

When no cycle path is available, ride as close to the right edge as practical — but not in the gutter. Maintain about 50cm (roughly an arm's length) from the edge or parked cars. This keeps you out of the door zone and gives room to avoid potholes without swerving into traffic.

Door zone: Stay at least 1m from parked vehicles. Don't weave in and out of parking bays — pick a steady line.

When Drivers Overtake You

Drivers must give you at least 1m in built-up areas, 1.5m outside. If the road is too narrow, they must wait — you're not obstructing traffic by being on the road.

Key Road Hazards

  • Uneven surfaces — potholes, cobblestones, cracked pavement can damage tyres or throw you off balance
  • Tram tracks — extremely slippery when wet; cross at 90 degrees; trams always have priority
  • Door zone — keep 1.5m from parked cars; look for signs someone's inside
  • Intersections — where most accidents happen; make eye contact, use hand signals
  • Weather — wet leaves, ice, and snow reduce grip; slow down, increase braking distance
  • Heavy traffic — ride defensively; assume drivers haven't seen you

Expect Rain

Belgium averages 12–15 rainy days per month. The trick is learning to ride safely in it, not avoiding it.

Use front and rear lights even during the day in grey or wet conditions. A high-vis vest is required by law when riding outside built-up areas. Keep lenses clean.

Delivery Hazards

Take Five Seconds to Assess

When you arrive at a delivery address, pause and look around. Is the path clear? Are there obvious hazards? A quick "Take 5" mental check can spot risks before they become problems.

Slips, Trips, and Falls

  • Slip hazards: Wet paving, moss-covered tiles, oily driveways — slow down and test your footing
  • Trip hazards: Loose stones, garden tools, toys, uneven slabs — keep eyes on the ground
  • Fall hazards: Staircases, steep steps, poorly lit entryways — use your phone's torch if needed

Risk increases significantly at dusk, night, or during winter. Wear non-slip shoes.

Dealing with Pets

⚠ Warning

If you see or hear an animal and you're not comfortable, contact the customer and ask them to secure the pet or meet you outside.

If facing an aggressive dog:

  • Do: Stay calm, keep quiet, use a firm low voice, stand upright, move to higher ground if possible
  • Don't: Panic, scream, run, flail arms, make direct eye contact, or jump up and down

In Belgium, dog owners are strictly liable for harm their animal causes. If bitten, report to police on 101 (or 112 in emergency). Notify Takeaway.com through the app.

Handling Abusive Behaviour

⚠ Warning

If someone is verbally or physically aggressive:

  1. Move to a safe distance immediately
  2. Stay calm — don't engage, argue, or retaliate
  3. Report it via the Courier App as soon as possible

If physically assaulted, call police on 101 (or 112 in emergency). File a report at the nearest police station. You have the right to feel safe while working.

Carry Emergency Contact Details

Keep emergency contact information on a physical card in your wallet or bag — not just on your phone. Paramedics and police may not be able to unlock your phone.

Hazard Awareness — The "Take 5" Method

As a courier, your work environment extends beyond the roads. Before undertaking any new task or facing changed conditions, run a quick hazard assessment.

Five Key Questions

  1. What are the potential hazards? (slippery surfaces, poorly lit entrances, aggressive animals, heavy packages, weather, road hazards)
  2. What risks do these hazards pose? (falls, injuries, exposure, theft, delays, confrontations)
  3. What measures can I take to control these risks? (non-slip shoes, flashlight, route planning, hand truck, weather checks, reduced speed)
  4. Do I have the necessary equipment and skills? (proper gear, working lights, area familiarity, handling ability)
  5. Am I confident I can perform this task safely? (If not, seek advice or assistance)

General Considerations

  • Building access: Be aware of intercoms, key codes, and secure entrances
  • Customer interactions: Maintain professionalism; be aware of communication barriers
  • Weather: Be prepared for changing conditions
  • Personal safety: Be aware of surroundings, especially during late-night deliveries or in unfamiliar areas
  • Package handling: Be aware of weight and size; use appropriate lifting techniques

Manual Handling — Protecting Your Back

Think Before You Lift

Assess the bag's weight, shape, and stability. Plan your path — are there steps, uneven surfaces, or obstacles?

Lifting Technique

✔ Safety Tip
  1. Keep it close — hold the bag as close to your waist as possible; distance multiplies strain on your lower back
  2. Stable base — feet shoulder-width apart for balance
  3. Grip properly — firm, secure hold with both hands
  4. Bend your knees, not your back — let your leg muscles do the work
  5. Keep your back straight — neutral, upright position throughout
  6. Don't twist — move your feet to change direction; keep hips and shoulders aligned
  7. Look ahead — head up, eyes forward for balance and posture

Know Your Limits

European standards suggest a reference weight of 15kg for the general working population, up to 25kg under optimal conditions. If a bag feels too heavy, ask the restaurant to split the order or contact support.

Listen to Your Body

Pain is your body telling you something is wrong. If your back aches or shoulders feel tight, take a break. Ignoring early warning signs turns minor strains into serious long-term injuries.

Support When You Need It

Experiencing pain? Use live chat in the app. If you sustain a back injury, seek medical attention and report it.

Delivering in Hot Weather

Belgium's Summers Are Getting Hotter

Heat waves have become more frequent and intense, with temperatures above 38°C in recent summers. Belgium activates its national "Ozone and Heat Plan" between May and September. People are now exposed to over 50 hours of heat stress conditions annually — 66% more than in the 1990s.

The Risks

  • UV exposure — severe sunburn and skin cancer risk; couriers on bikes and motorbikes are particularly exposed
  • Dehydration — headache is an early symptom; by the time you feel thirsty, you're already dehydrated
  • Heat stress — extreme tiredness, muscle cramps, nausea, vomiting
  • Heat stroke — medical emergency; body overheats and can't regulate temperature; can be fatal

Recognising Warning Signs

Heavy sweating (or hot, dry skin), paleness or flushed skin, headache, dizziness, extreme tiredness, muscle cramps, nausea, or vomiting. If you notice any of these, stop delivering immediately.

What to Do: Heat Stress

Move to shade or indoors. Remove excess clothing. Drink water slowly and steadily. Cool down with cold water on forehead, neck, and arms. Rest. If symptoms don't improve after 30 minutes, contact your GP.

What to Do: Heat Stroke

⚠ Warning

Hot, dry skin; confusion; loss of consciousness; rapid pulse — call 112 immediately. While waiting: remove excess clothing, use fans, apply wet cloths. Do not give ice-cold water or plunge into cold water — this can cause shock.

Prevention

✔ Safety Tip
  • Wear lightweight, light-coloured, long-sleeved clothing — it protects better from UV than bare skin
  • Apply SPF 30+ sunscreen every 2–4 hours; don't skip neck, ears, and backs of hands
  • Drink water regularly throughout your run — carry a bottle and refill whenever possible
  • Take regular breaks in shade or air-conditioning
  • Check if your medications increase heat sensitivity — ask your doctor or pharmacist
  • Monitor RMI heat warnings before your run

Support When You Need It

Struggling with heat? Use live chat in the app. In a heat-related emergency, call 112. For heat stress (not heat stroke), contact your GP first.
